Accuracy diagnostics

Sends one row per scored product to the shared database, on its own tab. Each row carries the ingredient text that was read, where it came from, which rules fired, and the score that resulted — enough to judge whether a score was right, rather than only noticing when one looks wrong. Batched.

What leaves your computer

Scoring itself is entirely local: the rules, your weights and every score are computed in your browser, and there is no analytics and no telemetry.

  • Shared online database (on by default) — the store and product IDs on a search page, and what was read off products you score, go to the Ingredient Check Apps Script. The spreadsheet behind it is private. Never your settings.
  • Rate every search result (on by default) and Score on hover — request product pages from amazon.com using your normal signed-in session, so Amazon sees them much as it would if you had opened the products yourself. Three at a time, at most 60 per results page, and only for products not already answered.
  • Open Food Facts fallback — sends a barcode to world.openfoodfacts.org when a page has one but no readable ingredients. The barcode and nothing else.
  • Back up my settings (on by default) — Chrome's own sync, for your Chrome account only. Sign in with Google stores a copy on the Ingredient Check database keyed by a hash of your account id, only when you click it.
  • Send certifications and "Looks wrong?" reports — product identifiers and certification text. Never anything about you.
  • Accuracy diagnostics — one row per scored product. This one does record which products were scored; see the section above.

Read label photos makes no network request of its own beyond fetching the product image the page already shows: the OCR engine ships with the extension and runs on your computer.

Turn off the shared database, the backup and Rate every search result, leave the other boxes off, and the extension makes no network requests of its own.

What a score is, and is not

A score reads the ingredient list against a rulebase and your weights. It is a guide to what is written on the label, not a health assessment, and it is not medical, dietary or allergy advice. Two things follow from how it works:

  • It can only judge what it can read. When too little of a list matches anything the rulebase knows, no score is shown at all rather than a confident guess.
  • Amazon's ingredient text is sometimes incomplete, sometimes out of date, and sometimes for a different size or flavour of the product. The package is the authority.
